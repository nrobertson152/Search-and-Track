# Taking the Watch

> What the job is, and how a shift goes

You command an air defense sector's weapons room for one shift at a time. You do not fly anything and you do not see anything directly. You have radars that report what they can, telephone lines and data links that sometimes work, interceptors on alert, missile batteries, and a higher headquarters that wants answers and gives permission slowly.

Your job is to know what is in your airspace, to say what it is, and to deal with it within the rules. Most of what you will see is harmless. The skill is in finding the exceptions quickly, without shooting at the harmless.

## Two services, one simulation

**NORAD / Air Defense Command (United States).** You sit in a SAGE Direction Center. Radar sites send their plots to the AN/FSQ-7 computer every scan over data lines. The computer associates plots with tracks; you start tracks with the light gun, ask height finders for altitudes, and identify tracks against flight plans and IFF. Interceptors are flown by voice, or by data link once the F-106 arrives.

**PVO Strany (Soviet Union).** You sit in an air defense army command post. Radar companies telephone their targets in to the plotting board every minute or so, and each report is two and a half to four and a half minutes old when it is plotted. Guidance points, each with its own radar, steer interceptors in real time. The army command post decides; the guidance points execute.

Both services fight the same physics. The radar equation, the horizon, clutter, jamming, fuel and the weather are identical. Doctrine, equipment and the flow of information differ, and so does the way a shift feels.

## The core loop

1. **Detect.** A return appears on a radar: a plot on the display, a blip on the scope, a telephoned report.
2. **Track.** Start a track (US light gun) or wait for a target number (PVO). Keep tracks clean: drop phantoms, merge duplicates.
3. **Height.** Search radars measure range and bearing. Ask a height finder (US) or read the V-beam estimate (PVO).
4. **Identify.** IFF, flight plans, schedules, behaviour. When none of these explain a track, it is an UNKNOWN.
5. **Commit.** Scramble interceptors with the right task and geometry. Watch the predicted intercept point and the fuel.
6. **Identify by eye.** Most intercepts end with a pilot reading a tail number, not with a weapon.
7. **Act within the rules.** Weapons need authority. Ask for it, with evidence.
8. **Recover.** Bring the fighters home before they run dry.

All the while you keep the network healthy: radars fail, lines go down, the computer has a bad half, and alerts pile up waiting for acknowledgement.

## A shift, start to finish

From the title screen choose a mode. **Campaign** is a career: the watches of your service, in historical order. **Scenarios** lets you take any historical watch. **Alert Duty** generates a fresh shift on a real order of battle. **Training** has three instructor-led lessons per side; start there.

Every shift opens with a **briefing**: the situation, intelligence, forces and objectives, typed up on the duty officer's desk. Press TAKE THE WATCH when you are ready.

The shift ends when its time runs out, when its objectives are settled, or when you end it from the menu. The **debrief** replays the shift, shows what was really in the air, scores the objectives and prints the next morning's newspaper.

## If you are new

Take **Training 1** first. The instructor's card is pinned to your display: it tells you what to do next, circles the controls to use, and marks the aircraft or site it means. A step completes when you have done what it asks.

Then read the chapters on **Radar** and **Tracks and Identification**. Everything else follows from those two.
