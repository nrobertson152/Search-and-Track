# Interception

> Alert states, geometry, fuel, and the last few miles

## Alert states

Squadrons hold aircraft at alert states measured in minutes: 5, 15, 30 or 60 on the US side, readiness 1, 2 or 3 in the PVO. A five-minute aircraft is airborne three or four minutes after the order reaches the base; the order itself travels over a line that has its own delay, and may be down. RAISE ALERT brings one more aircraft to the best state, which takes time and tires crews. Keep some aircraft on alert at the end of the shift: the next watch needs them.

## Scramble

On the Weapons console choose the number of aircraft (×1 or ×2), the **task** and the **geometry**, select the target track, and press SCRAMBLE. A flight already airborne can be committed to a new track with ASSIGN.

**Tasks**

- **INTERCEPT**: commit to attack geometry. Weapons still need clearance.
- **ID PASS**: a stern approach that lets the pilots read markings without frightening anyone.
- **SHADOW**: identify, then follow.
- **CAP**: orbit over a point and wait.

**Geometry**

- **LEAD**: lead-collision course. The fastest intercept, ending head-on or on the beam: for forward-firing rockets.
- **STERN**: stern conversion. The flight rolls out behind the target: for rear-aspect missiles, guns, and identification.
- **PURSUIT**: point at the target. Simple, slow, and it burns fuel.

## Control

The ground computes the **predicted intercept point** (the X on the display), the time to intercept, and the fuel needed to come home. Watch both.

- **Voice control.** Each vector reaches the pilot several seconds after you give it.
- **Data link (US).** From the F-106 onward, SAGE flies the intercept by data link: no voice delay, but only while the computer is up.
- **Guidance points (PVO).** Each guidance point steers the flights on its own scope, in real time. Hand a flight to the next point with GUID. PT when it leaves the first one's coverage. A flight whose guidance point cannot see the target is steered from the plotting board, on reports minutes old.
- **Manual vectors.** Switch a flight to MANUAL and give it headings, altitudes and afterburner yourself.

## The last few miles

As the flight closes, the leader's own radar searches for the target: **contact**. Then the pilots see it: **tally**. Interceptor radars have short ranges, and the early ones cannot look down into ground clutter. Night and cloud shorten the visual range.

A **visual identification** is reported on the radio and in the comms log: type, markings, and often altitude.

A jet closing on a slow aircraft overshoots. It has to slow down and come in from behind, and an identification pass on a light aircraft takes patience.

## Forcing an aircraft to land

FORCE LANDING puts the flight in close formation with an identified aircraft and signals it to follow and land: wing rocking, lights. The signals need steady formation for a minute by day and two at night, and a jet cannot hold formation on a much slower aircraft. Some crews follow; some ignore you; some turn and run.

WARNING SHOTS fires a burst across the target's path from alongside. At night the crew may not see it.

## Fuel

Afterburner (BUSTER) is faster and burns fuel three to five times faster. **Bingo fuel** is the fuel needed to get home: past it, the leader calls bingo and turns for base whatever you want. A fighter that runs dry is lost, however well the intercept went.

## Weapons

A flight fires only when **WPN FREE** is set and the rules of engagement allow it (see Rules of Engagement). Rockets and early missiles need the right geometry and range; guns need the flight close behind. The F-89J, F-101B and F-106A can carry the MB-1 Genie, a nuclear rocket: **special weapons** need special release from higher authority, then SPECIAL WPNS ARM.

ORDER RAMMING exists in PVO doctrine as a last resort, with the command post's permission.
