# Surface-to-Air Missiles

> Batteries, battalions and BOMARC

## The engagement sequence

1. **Readiness.** A unit not at readiness needs time to come up: BRING TO READINESS starts the clock.
2. **Designation.** Select the target track, then DESIGNATE it to the battery on the Missiles console. The order goes over the battery's line. A battery accepts a target only within about 1.3 times the range of its acquisition radar.
3. **Acquisition.** The battery's own radar searches the designated area and locks the strongest return it finds. That is not necessarily the aircraft you meant.
4. **Engagement.** When the target is inside the engagement zone (between minimum and maximum range, above minimum altitude), the battery fires its salvo.
5. **Missile flight.** Missiles are simulated objects with their own speed, flight time and lethal radius. They can miss.
6. **Assessment and re-engagement.** After a salvo the battery reports whether the target continues. With re-engagement ON ORDER it waits for you; with AUTO RE-ENG it fires again, which is wasteful against debris.
7. **Reload.** Launchers are reloaded from the battery's stock, which takes minutes.

HOLD FIRE stops an engagement; CANCEL drops the designation. SALVO sets the number of missiles per engagement.

## Debris

A kill leaves debris that keeps painting on radar for a few minutes. "Target destroyed" is an assessment, not a certainty, and a battery on automatic re-engagement may fire at the wreckage.

## United States

- **Nike Ajax and Nike Hercules** are US Army batteries around cities and bases, coordinated through the Army Air Defense Command Post (AADCP, later the Missile Master). The AADCP **holds fire when friendly fighters are near the target**.
- **Hawk** is a low-altitude missile of the later 1960s.
- **BOMARC** is an Air Force area-defense missile launched under SAGE control with the Senior Director's key, guided to the track by the computer until its own seeker takes over. It is nuclear-armed: launching needs special release.

## Soviet Union

- **S-25** ringed Moscow from the mid-1950s.
- **S-75** battalions defend the cities and industry of the 1960s: a P-12 for acquisition, the SNR-75 for guidance, six launchers.
- **S-125** for low altitude, **S-200** for long range, **S-300PT** from the late 1970s.
- In 1960 there is **no automatic deconfliction** between fighters and missile battalions. Only IFF codes and orders protect your own fighters; a squadron on the wrong monthly code looks foreign to a battalion.

## Exercise rules

In an exercise, launches are simulated. The umpires assess every engagement and the target flies on, scored. A simulated attack on a civil aircraft is still a violation.
