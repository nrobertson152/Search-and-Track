# Battles and the War

> Whole air battles fought by both sides, the staff that runs them, and wars that remember

## What a battle is

A battle is the Cold War turned hot: a hypothetical night in 1987 when the raids come for real. Both sides fight on their own. Enemy bombers, jammers, shadowers, escorts and cruise missiles fly their missions; ships steam in formation, turn away from missiles and fire chaff; missile battalions and ship fire channels pick their targets; your interceptors are committed, relieved and recalled by your staff. Everything works from the radar picture: what the radars do not see, nobody can shoot.

A battle runs in phases — the search, the electronic attack, the raid, the counterstrike — and lasts about an hour of game time, fifteen minutes at the default 4×. Each phase opens when the situation calls for it (or when nothing has happened for a while), so the story keeps moving. Inside the phases nothing is scripted: who finds whom and who gets through is computed.

It ends when the last raiders have gone home, when the time runs out, or when you lose what you were there to protect. The debrief gives VICTORY, DRAW or DEFEAT, the score against the objectives, what each side lost, every decision and who made it, and a newspaper.

## Your staff

The staff runs the routine so you can command:

- **Identification** by the battle rules: a fast mover without IFF, a jammer, or anything coming from enemy territory is hostile; our own aircraft are recognised by data link.
- **Commitment**: the worst threats get interceptors, as far out as the posture allows, while a reserve stays on alert. Bombers and missile carriers come first; a fighter sweep far out is not chased.
- **Patrols** are manned, relieved when fuel runs low, and return to station after a fight.
- **Self-defence**: flights turn on fighters that come for them — but not when they are about to shoot the bomber they were sent for.
- **Missiles**: every fire channel gets a target, incoming missiles first.
- **Radar aircraft** run from fighters that are closing on them.

## Commanding

The **BATTLE (F7)** console is your desk.

- **Standing orders.** POSTURE (aggressive, balanced, defensive) sets how far out the staff commits. FIGHTERS on AUTO lets the staff commit; MANUAL leaves it to you. MISSILES: FREE, TIGHT (only close to what you defend) or HOLD. RADARS: RADIATE or SILENT (emission control for the ships). RESERVE: how many aircraft stay on alert.
- **Priority targets.** Right-click a track and choose PRIORITY: the staff commits on it first, at any range.
- **Decisions.** Some calls are yours: push the patrols out, launch the strike, go silent, move the radar aircraft. Each comes as a card with a timer; if you do not answer, the staff takes the default.
- **Reinforcements**, when higher command offers them.
- Everything you can do in a watch, you can still do: right-click orders, scrambles, vectors, SAM assignments.

Time compression runs up to 32×; **ADVANCE** (or `Enter`) runs at 96× until something important happens. In battles the speed drops by itself to 2× for anything hot — a kill, a hit, a missile warning — and comes back after it.

## Watching

**SPECTATE** on the briefing lets the staff fight the whole battle while you watch; TAKE COMMAND hands it to you at any moment. **UMPIRE VIEW** shows both sides as they really are. Click any unit — a flight, a group, a ship — for its card and its story so far; **FOLLOW** keeps the display on it. After the battle, **WATCH REPLAY** plays it back with the truth: every aircraft, every missile, every hit, at up to 120×.

## The ground war

On the Soviet side the Americans come for the ground as well as the fleet.

- **Cruise missiles** (Tomahawk, ALCM) fly preplanned routes 30-60 m over the terrain, pop up and dive on airfields, radars and missile battalions. Ground radars see them late: their horizon is short. The A-50 and the MiG-31's look-down radar see them early, and interceptors can hunt them.
- **Anti-radiation missiles** (HARM) home on any radar that is radiating, and coast on its last position if it goes quiet. Battalion crews switch off when one is reported inbound: the missile usually misses, but the battalion does not shoot either. Kill the Hornets that carry them.
- What is hit stays hit: a destroyed radar leaves a hole in the picture; a cratered runway launches nobody until the craters are filled (ten to forty minutes); a battalion without its fire-control radar fires no more. The display marks them.

## Emission control

A radar that radiates can be heard far beyond the range at which it sees. Shadowers and electronic-intelligence aircraft find a fleet that radiates; a fleet that is silent has to be found by radar, from much closer. Under emission control the ships' search radars are off and their missile fire channels stay silent except against a missile closing on the ship itself. The radar aircraft and the coastal radars keep the picture. When the raid comes, you will be asked to light them off.

Missile carriers need a fix to fire from long range. With a fresh fix they launch from maximum range; with an old one they press in closer; with nothing but the briefing's guess they have to come in close, into your fighters and missiles. A ship that has to fire on a fix will not fire at all without one.

## The war

**THE WAR** on the title screen is a short campaign of battles fought by the same forces, night after night.

- Aircraft shot down stay lost; replacements arrive between battles.
- Damaged ships are only partly repaired; a crippled ship is in the dockyard; a sunk one is gone. A war can end early if you lose what the force cannot fight without.
- Radars that were destroyed stay destroyed.
- The enemy's losses carry over too. A regiment cut in half on the first night comes back at half strength; an enemy cruiser put out of action fires no missiles the next night.

The war room shows the war diary, what is left of the order of battle and the next battle. Each battle of a war can also be played on its own from BATTLES, starting from the losses its scenario assumes.

## Difficulty

Set in SETTINGS, applied when a watch begins. RECRUIT: every crew position works on its own, alert aircraft get airborne faster, battle decisions wait twice as long and the enemy's raids come a quarter smaller. VETERAN: in the historical watches every position is yours to run by hand; in battles decisions come with less time and the raids are a quarter larger. The physics — detection, tracking, kill chances — are the same at every level.

## The battles

| Battle | Side | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| The Outer Air Battle | US | Bering Sea: BEAR D shadowers, BADGER jammers, two squadrons of BACKFIREs against the Carl Vinson group; an alpha strike on the FRUNZE. |
| The Second Raid | US | The second night: the regiment's survivors and a fresh squadron down the Bering Strait; the FRUNZE closing with Granits. |
| The Northern Bastion | PVO | Kola: Tomahawks over Finnmark, a carrier strike with HARM shooters and jammers on the bases and the KIROV group, then B-52s with Harpoons. |
| The Second Strike | PVO | The second night: ALCMs from the north, Tomcats sent deep for the A-50, then the second strike. |

All of them are hypothetical. The forces, sites and weapons are approximately right for 1987; the war is not.
